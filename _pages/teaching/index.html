Teaching Philosophy

I discovered my love for biology at a young age — a love that became a passion because of enthusiastic, engaged teachers. As a teacher, I strive to cultivate that same love of science and learning in my own students. My teaching philosophy rests on a few core principles:

Inquiry-based learning. I pose broad scientific questions and ask students to design experiments or ideas that answer them, rather than teaching by rote memorization. The silences at the start can be uncomfortable — I remind students I felt the same way once, and that I’m genuinely excited to hear their ideas. Students quickly become active, confident participants, and my exams reflect this same emphasis on understanding over memorization.

Teamwork. I build group discussion and problem-solving into coursework so students learn from each other and experience the value of collaboration — with early check-ins and individual contribution plans to keep the load evenly shared.

The power of “I don’t know.” I model intellectual honesty for my students: if I don’t know an answer, I say so, and bring the answer back to the next class. Being explicit about the unknown motivates curiosity rather than discouraging it.

An approachable learning environment. Students learn differently, so I vary formats — lectures, group discussions, smaller teams, written exercises — even in large classrooms, and give quieter students structured, advance notice ways to participate and lead. I also make a point of representing the contributions of scholars from marginalized communities in course material.

I’ve taught microbiology lab courses to a broad mix of majors and experience levels, and an honors-level cell biology course where I taught undergraduates to read, analyze, and design their own scientific questions — experience that continues to shape how I teach today.
