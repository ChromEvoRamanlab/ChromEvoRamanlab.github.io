We study what drives the innovation of essential epigenetic factors. <br>
Our research program focuses on revealing **when and how epigenetic factors change across evolution and what the influence of these changes are on fundamental biological processes** We combine evolutionary and phylogenetic analyses with molecular and cell biology, genetics, and genomics in multiple model organisms to uncover the causes and consequences of chromatin innovation — and the surprising plasticity it displays across both evolution and disease.

## Discovering chromatin innovations

Eukaryotic histone repertoires are built from canonical histones (H2A, H2B, H3, and H4) and their variants. Although histones are among the most conserved proteins in biology, our work has repeatedly found the opposite pattern hiding in plain sight:

- Centromeric proteins in *Caenorhabditis* nematodes show an unusually high rate of evolutionary turnover, which we think reflects unique challenges posed by their holocentric chromosomes.
- Mammalian germ cells contain previously unrecognized H2B histone variants that turn over rapidly across evolution, including one variant that arose in vertebrates but was later lost in rodents.

In on going work, we are studying H2A variant innovations in Diptera and fungi, using _Drosophila_ and _S. cereviseae_ as our lab models.<br>

In the Raman lab, we will expand beyond histones to study innovations across other chromatin proteins. For example, we analyzed EZHIP, a natural inhibitor of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 and a mimic of an oncohistone mutation, which shows dramatic divergence and is under positive selection. We hypothesize that these changes are potentially driven by parental genetic conflict during mammalian pregnancy. We are excited to continue to study chromatin proteins using this evolutionary lens in the lab and in collaborations.

## Functional consequences of chromatin innovation

Discovery is only half the story! we also test what these evolutionary changes actually do. Using engineered yeast strains that recreate fly-like histone repertoires, we've shown that combining features of two histone variants can measurably impair the DNA damage response, even while leaving normal cell growth intact. In flies, we've found that losing the H2Av variant severely disrupts fertility and oocyte maturation, and leads to increased transposon activity — suggesting a role for this chromatin innovation in protecting the genome during reproduction.

### Where the lab is headed

Building on this foundation, the lab is expanding in two directions:

1. **Beyond histones.** We are extending our evolutionary lens to other chromatin-associated proteins — including DNA damage response factors, chromatin remodelers, and chromatin modifiers — to map the co-evolutionary landscape of the proteins that package and regulate the genome.
2. **From evolution to disease.** We are studying the consequences of disease-associated chromatin mutations, starting with "oncohistones" (histone mutations recurrently found in cancers). Notably, some of these same mutations occur naturally in non-human species. By recreating and experimentally evolving these mutations in yeast and flies, we aim to uncover the compensatory changes that can accompany them — offering a basic-biology window into how these mutations behave in cancer evolution.

Across all of these projects, our approach is the same: use evolution as a discovery tool, and rigorous functional experiments to find out what the evolutionary signal actually means for the cell.
